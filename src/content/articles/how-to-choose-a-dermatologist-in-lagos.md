## 1. Check qualifications and registration

A dermatologist is a medical doctor who has completed postgraduate specialist training in skin, hair and nail disease. In Nigeria, look for:

- **A medical degree** (MBBS, MBChB or equivalent).
- **Current registration** with the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN).
- **A specialist fellowship** with dermatology training, such as FMCP (National Postgraduate Medical College of Nigeria) or FWACP (West African College of Physicians), or a recognised overseas qualification.
- **Membership of a professional body,** such as the Nigerian Association of Dermatologists, which is a sign of engagement with the specialty.

Titles such as "skin therapist", "skin consultant" and "skincare specialist" are not medical qualifications. If you are unsure, ask the clinic who will see you and what their qualifications are.

## 2. Look for relevant experience

Ask whether the doctor regularly treats your particular concern, and how conditions and treatments are adapted for brown and Black skin. This matters most for pigment disorders, keloids, hair loss in African hair, and procedures such as peels and lasers, where settings that suit light skin can harm dark skin.

If your concern is in a child, during pregnancy, or involves hair or nails, ask whether the clinic sees those patients routinely.

## 3. Notice how the consultation is conducted

A sound dermatology consultation includes:

- **Questions first:** how long, what you have tried, your general health, medicines, products and family history.
- **A proper examination** in good light, often with a dermatoscope, and of more than the single spot when relevant.
- **Tests when needed:** scraping, swab, blood tests or biopsy, with an explanation of why.
- **A named diagnosis,** or an honest statement that more information is needed.
- **A plan you understand:** what each medicine is, how to use it, for how long, and what side effects to watch for.
- **Realistic timelines.** Most skin treatments take weeks to months.
- **A follow-up date.**

## 4. Ask practical questions

- Where is the clinic, and how long will it take to get there at appointment time?
- What are the opening hours? Are weekend appointments available?
- How do I book, reschedule or ask a question between visits?
- Is an online consultation possible for follow-up?
- Are tests and procedures done on site?
- Does the clinic accept my health insurance, and how are fees communicated before treatment?
- Will I see the same doctor each time?

## 5. Check what is dispensed

Medicines should come in their original, labelled packaging with a NAFDAC registration number, batch number and expiry date. Be cautious of creams in plain jars, products "mixed" for you without a list of ingredients, and injections or drips whose contents are not named.

## 6. Know the warning signs

- No clear answer about who the doctor is or their qualifications
- Treatment sold before your skin has been examined
- Promises of a cure, a lighter complexion or results within days
- Pressure to pay for a package immediately
- Lightening injections or drips
- Procedures in a non-clinical setting
- No explanation of risks, and no aftercare or contact for problems

## 7. Use reviews and recommendations sensibly

A recommendation from someone with a similar concern is useful. Online reviews tell you about waiting times, communication and staff, but individual results vary and before-and-after pictures can mislead. Weigh them alongside the points above, not in place of them.

> [!DERM] When to see a dermatologist
> - A skin, hair or nail problem has lasted more than two weeks or keeps returning
> - You have used several creams without a diagnosis
> - You have a changing mole, hair loss, or marks and scars you want treated
> - You are considering a peel, laser or injectable treatment
