## What is the difference between lightening your skin and treating dark patches?

The two are often confused, and the difference decides the answer.

**Treating hyperpigmentation** means returning a darker patch, such as a mark left by acne or a patch of melasma, to your own natural colour. A dermatologist does this with a diagnosis, sunscreen and a cream prescribed at a known strength for a set period, on the affected area only. Used this way, the medicines have a long safety record.

**Skin lightening or bleaching** means making normal skin lighter than its natural colour, usually over the whole face or body, for months or years, with products bought without a prescription. There is no safe way to do this. Your natural shade is set by your genes, and holding it lighter requires continuous use of a strong chemical over a large area.

Nigeria has one of the highest reported rates of skin lightening product use in the world, according to the World Health Organization. This article is not a judgement on anyone who has used them. It explains what the products are and what doctors see as a result.

## What is in lightening creams and soaps?

Three ingredients account for most of the effect and most of the harm.

- **Hydroquinone** blocks pigment production. It is a legitimate prescription treatment for patches of pigment. In cosmetic products it appears at high or undeclared strengths, and long use causes irritation, a halo of lighter skin, and ochronosis.
- **Potent corticosteroids,** such as clobetasol and betamethasone, lighten skin as a side effect. They are meant for short courses on inflamed skin. Many lightening creams, and the "triple action" creams used as cosmetics, contain them.
- **Mercury** salts lighten quickly. Mercury is banned from cosmetics in Nigeria and most countries, but still turns up in imported and unlabelled creams and soaps.

Other products mix these with strong acids. Creams that are "mixed" to order in shops and markets are the most unpredictable of all, because no one knows what is in the jar.

## Skin bleaching side effects: what the creams do over time

**In the first months,** the skin looks lighter and smoother. Then problems appear, usually in this order:

1. Uneven tone: light face, dark knuckles, elbows, knees and feet.
2. Acne-like bumps, fine facial hair and a shiny, thin look.
3. Stretch marks on the inner thighs, upper arms and breasts, wider than those of pregnancy.
4. Fungal rashes and scabies that keep coming back because the skin's defences are suppressed.
5. Easy bruising, tears from minor knocks and slow-healing wounds.
6. Blue-black, sooty patches on the cheeks and temples: ochronosis, which is very difficult to reverse.

**Inside the body,** steroid absorbed through large areas of skin can raise blood pressure and blood sugar and suppress the body's own steroid production. Mercury damages the kidneys and nervous system; signs include swelling of the legs, tremor, numbness and mood change. Surgeons and obstetricians see wounds that will not hold stitches in long-term users.

## Are pills, drips and glutathione injections safer than creams?

No. Glutathione is an antioxidant the body makes. Small studies of tablets show a slight lightening in some people that fades when the tablets stop. For injections and intravenous drips, good evidence of benefit is lacking and the risks are real: severe allergic reactions, serious skin reactions, kidney and liver injury, and infections including hepatitis from shared or unsterile equipment. Regulators in several countries, including NAFDAC, have warned against injectable lightening products.

Drips are also frequently mixed with other substances, such as high-dose vitamin C or unlabelled steroids, so you cannot know what is entering your bloodstream.

## Why does skin get darker when I stop?

Steroids and hydroquinone hold pigment cells back. When they are withdrawn, the cells rebound, and the skin, now thin and inflamed, reacts to sun more than before. Burning, redness and darkening in the first weeks are common and frightening, and they are the main reason people restart. A planned withdrawal with a dermatologist makes this stage shorter and safer, and treats the acne, fungal infection or eczema that the cream was masking.

## What are safer ways to even out my skin tone?

- **Daily sunscreen.** Uneven tone is mostly sun plus inflammation. See [Do Black people need sunscreen?](/blog/do-black-people-need-sunscreen/)
- **Treat the cause** of dark marks: acne, eczema, friction or hair removal. Start with [how to get rid of dark spots on the face](/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-dark-spots-on-your-face/).
- **Gentle brighteners** such as niacinamide, azelaic acid and vitamin C.
- **A diagnosis** when your face is darker than your body or one area will not match; several conditions cause this, as explained in [Why is my face darker than my body?](/blog/why-is-my-face-darker-than-my-body/)
- **Dermatologist-led treatment** for melasma and stubborn patches, including [chemical peels](/services/aesthetic-treatments/chemical-peels/) chosen for dark skin.

## How do I check a product?

- Is there a full ingredient list and a NAFDAC registration number?
- Does it contain hydroquinone, or names ending in -sone, -solone or -nide (steroids)?
- Does it list mercury, mercurous chloride, calomel or "Hg"?
- Does it promise results in days, or a specific number of shades?
- Was it mixed for you, or sold without a label?

If the answer to any of the last four is yes, do not use it.
