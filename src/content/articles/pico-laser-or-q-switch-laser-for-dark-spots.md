## How do pigment lasers work?

Pigment absorbs laser light. If the pulse is short enough, the pigment particle heats and shatters before the heat can spread to the surrounding skin. The fragments are then cleared by the body's immune cells over the following weeks.

- **Q-switched lasers** produce pulses lasting nanoseconds (billionths of a second). They have been used for decades on pigment and tattoos.
- **Pico lasers** produce pulses lasting picoseconds (trillionths of a second). The effect is more mechanical, like a tap that cracks the particle, and less thermal.

Both types are available at several wavelengths. For darker skin tones, **1064 nm** is the workhorse, because this longer wavelength passes the pigment-rich surface layer with less absorption and so carries less risk of burning the epidermis. Shorter wavelengths, such as 532 nm, are absorbed strongly by surface melanin and are used with great caution, if at all, on brown and Black skin.

## What do they have in common?

- The same targets: sun spots, some birthmarks, certain kinds of deep pigment and tattoo ink.
- Short sessions with little or no downtime at low energies.
- The same main risk on dark skin: inflammation that leaves new dark or pale marks.
- The need for sunscreen before and after.

## Where do they differ?

| | Q-switched | Pico |
|---|---|---|
| Pulse length | Nanoseconds | Picoseconds |
| Main effect on pigment | Heat and shock wave | Mostly shock wave |
| Heat to nearby skin | More | Less |
| Tattoos | Effective; more sessions for some colours | Often fewer sessions, better on some stubborn inks |
| Track record | Longest | Shorter but growing |

Some pico devices add a lens that creates tiny zones of injury to stimulate collagen, used for acne scars and texture.

## Which dark spots respond to laser?

- **Good candidates:** sun spots (lentigines), freckle-like spots, certain birthmarks such as naevus of Ota, and tattoos.
- **Sometimes, with care:** stubborn marks left by inflammation, and melasma that has not responded to creams and peels. Low-energy "laser toning" is used, results vary, and relapse is common.
- **Poor candidates:** active acne marks that are still forming, melasma as a first treatment, and darkening caused by a product or medicine that is still being used.
- **Different treatment needed:** raised spots such as [dermatosis papulosa nigra](/conditions/dermatosis-papulosa-nigra/) are removed by cautery; moles need examination, not laser.

This is why diagnosis comes first. Lasering a spot without knowing what it is can waste money, worsen pigment, or hide a problem that needed a biopsy.

## What happens during treatment?

After a consultation and photographs, a small test area is treated and reviewed in two to four weeks. If your skin responds well, the full area is treated with eye protection and cooling. Skin may look slightly darker or feel rough for a few days before it lightens. You will be asked to avoid sun, heat, scrubs and active skincare for about a week and to use sunscreen daily.

## So which should I choose?

Choose the clinician, not the machine. The right questions are: What are my spots? Is laser the right first step, or should creams and [peels](/services/aesthetic-treatments/chemical-peels/) come first? Which wavelength will you use on my skin, and will you do a test area? What happens if I darken afterwards?
