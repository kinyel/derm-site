  - q: Which laser is best for Black skin?

## How lasers work on skin

A laser produces light of a single colour, or wavelength. Each wavelength is absorbed by a particular target: the pigment in a hair, the melanin in a dark spot, the ink in a tattoo, the red of a blood vessel or the water in skin tissue. The target heats or shatters while the skin around it is left as cool as possible. Choosing a laser means matching the wavelength, the length of each pulse and the energy to the target and to your skin tone.

Light-based devices such as IPL and LED are not lasers. IPL sends a broad band of light and is less selective; LED delivers gentle light that does not heat the skin.

## The clinic's laser technologies

The clinic uses Q-switched, pico, Erbium YAG and fractional CO2 laser technologies. Each has its own page explaining what it is and what it is used for:

- **[Q-switched laser](/services/laser-treatments/q-switch-laser/):** very short pulses that break up pigment and tattoo ink.
- **[Pico laser](/services/laser-treatments/pico-laser/):** even shorter pulses, measured in trillionths of a second, for pigment and tattoos.
- **[Erbium YAG laser](/services/laser-treatments/erbium-yag-laser/):** removes very thin layers of skin to resurface scars and texture.
- **Fractional CO2 laser:** treats columns of skin for scars and resurfacing. See [laser skin resurfacing](/services/laser-treatments/laser-skin-resurfacing/).

Our article compares [pico and Q-switch lasers for dark spots](/blog/pico-laser-or-q-switch-laser-for-dark-spots/).

## Why skin tone changes the plan

In light skin there is little melanin to compete with the target. In brown and Black skin the epidermis is full of it, and it absorbs the same light. Too much energy, the wrong wavelength or too little cooling causes burns, blisters, dark marks or pale patches that can take months to settle or may be permanent.

The safeguards are practical:

1. **Assessment.** Your skin tone, tanning, medicines and history of keloids or cold sores are checked.
2. **Preparation.** Sun avoidance and sunscreen for weeks beforehand; sometimes a pigment-calming cream.
3. **Test patch.** A small hidden area is treated and reviewed before the first full treatment.
4. **Conservative settings and cooling,** increased only as your skin shows it can tolerate them.
5. **Aftercare and review.** Written instructions, strict sun protection and a check before the next treatment.

> [!DERM] Tell the dermatologist before laser if
> - you have had keloids, cold sores or a previous bad reaction to laser or a peel
> - you have been in strong sun or used a tanning product recently
> - you take or have recently taken acne tablets, or any medicine that makes skin sensitive to light
> - you are pregnant or breastfeeding
> - you have used a bleaching cream, which can change how skin reacts

## Laser is not always the answer

Some pigment problems respond better to prescription creams and peels, and [melasma](/conditions/melasma/) in particular can worsen with laser. Where a simpler treatment is likely to work as well with less risk, the dermatologist will say so.
