## What eczema looks like on brown and Black skin

Textbook eczema is described as red. On brown and Black skin a flare usually looks dark brown, grey, ashen or violet, and the inflammation is easier to feel, as warmth and roughness, than to see. Because redness is missed, eczema in dark skin is often judged milder than it is.

Features seen more often on dark skin:

- **Small bumps around hair follicles** that give the skin a goose-flesh texture, especially on the trunk and arms.
- **Thick, leathery patches** with deep skin lines where scratching has gone on for weeks, at the neck, wrists, ankles and behind the knees.
- **Dark marks or pale patches** left after a flare. These are temporary but can take months to even out; see [post-inflammatory hypopigmentation](/conditions/post-inflammatory-hypopigmentation/).
- **Dry, grey "ashy" skin** all over.
- **Darkening and fine folds under the eyes.**

Where it appears changes with age: cheeks and outer limbs in babies ([baby eczema](/conditions/baby-eczema/)), the creases of elbows and knees, neck, wrists and ankles in children, and the hands, eyelids, neck and flexures in adults.

## How to spot it

- Itch that is hard to ignore and worse at night
- Dry skin generally, with rougher, darker patches
- Flares that come and go over months or years
- Onset in childhood, or a personal or family history of asthma or hay fever
- Patches in the creases of the elbows and knees

A single ring-shaped patch with a raised, scaly edge and clearer centre is more likely to be ringworm. Our article [Ringworm or eczema: how to tell the difference](/blog/ringworm-or-eczema/) compares them.

## Causes and triggers

People with eczema have a weaker skin barrier, often inherited. The skin loses water easily and lets in irritants and allergens, and the immune system in the skin overreacts.

Triggers that matter in Lagos:

- **Harmattan:** cold, dry, dusty air pulls moisture from the skin. See [How to care for your skin during harmattan](/blog/how-to-care-for-your-skin-during-harmattan/).
- **Heat and sweat** for the rest of the year.
- **Antiseptic and medicated soaps,** hot water and long baths.
- **Scrubbing** with sponges and nets.
- **Fragranced creams and perfumes.**
- **Dust, cockroaches and pet dander.**
- **Infection** with staphylococcus bacteria.
- **Stress and lack of sleep.**

> [!DERM] See a dermatologist if
> - itching disturbs sleep or daily life
> - patches are not improving after two weeks of moisturiser
> - the skin is weeping, crusted yellow, or painful
> - eczema covers large areas or keeps returning
> - you have been using a steroid or mixed cream for more than a few weeks
> - you are not sure whether it is eczema or a fungal infection

> [!URGENT] Urgent care
> See a doctor the same day if eczema suddenly develops clusters of small, painful, punched-out sores or blisters, especially with fever. This can be eczema herpeticum, an infection with the cold sore virus that needs urgent treatment.

> [!AVOID] What to avoid
> - "Triple-action" and unlabelled mixed creams: they contain strong steroids, antifungals and antibiotics that are wrong for long-term use
> - Antiseptic liquid in bath water, and medicated or black soap on inflamed skin
> - Hot baths and vigorous scrubbing
> - Stopping a prescribed steroid cream too early out of fear, then letting the flare return
> - Herbal pastes and engine oil on open skin

## How it is diagnosed at the clinic

Eczema is diagnosed from the history and the appearance of the skin. Tests are used to rule out other causes or complications: a [skin scraping](/services/skin-tests-and-diagnostics/fungal-tests/) if ringworm is possible, a swab if infection is suspected, and [patch testing](/services/skin-tests-and-diagnostics/patch-testing/) when eczema on the hands, face or eyelids suggests [contact allergy](/conditions/contact-dermatitis/).

## Eczema treatment at the clinic

**Moisturisers, every day.** Thick, fragrance-free creams or ointments applied after bathing and again during the day, in generous amounts. This is treatment, not an optional extra.

**Anti-inflammatory creams for flares.** A steroid cream or ointment of the strength suited to the site, once or twice daily until the patch is flat and no longer itchy, then stopped or reduced as planned. Non-steroid creams (calcineurin inhibitors) are used on delicate areas such as the eyelids.

**Proactive treatment.** For patches that keep returning in the same place, applying the anti-inflammatory cream twice a week between flares prevents many relapses.

**Treating infection.** Weeping or crusted eczema is treated with antibiotics where needed.

**Help with sleep and itch.** Cooling the skin, keeping nails short and, sometimes, a short course of an antihistamine at night.

**For severe eczema:** [narrowband UVB phototherapy](/services/clinical-dermatology/phototherapy/), or tablets and injectable medicines that calm the immune response, with monitoring.

You leave with a written plan saying what to use where, how much and for how long. Medicines are dispensed by the clinic's [pharmacy](/services/dermatology-pharmacy/).

## Prevention and daily care

- Bathe once a day in lukewarm water for under ten minutes, with a mild, fragrance-free cleanser.
- Pat dry and moisturise within three minutes.
- Use your hands, not a sponge.
- Wear loose cotton; rinse laundry well.
- Keep fingernails short and smooth.
- Moisturise more during harmattan and in air-conditioned rooms.
- Treat flares on the first day, not the fifth.
