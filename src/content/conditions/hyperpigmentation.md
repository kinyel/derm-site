## What dark spots look like on brown and Black skin

Hyperpigmentation is a sign, not one disease. The pattern tells the dermatologist where it came from:

- **Marks the shape of what caused them:** round spots after pimples, lines after scratches, a patch where eczema or a burn healed. This is post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, by far the commonest type.
- **Symmetrical brown patches** on the cheeks, forehead and upper lip: [melasma patches](/conditions/melasma/).
- **Darker forehead, temples and cheekbones** than the rest of the face: sun, heat and friction; see [Why is my face darker than my body?](/blog/why-is-my-face-darker-than-my-body/)
- **Dark knuckles, elbows, knees, underarms and inner thighs:** friction, or a hormonal cause; see [dark underarms, inner thighs, knees and neck](/conditions/dark-underarms-inner-thighs-knees-and-neck/).
- **Blue-black or sooty patches on the cheeks and temples** after years of lightening creams: ochronosis; see [skin damage from bleaching and steroid creams](/conditions/skin-damage-from-bleaching-and-steroid-creams/).
- **Small raised dark dots** on the cheeks: [dermatosis papulosa nigra](/conditions/dermatosis-papulosa-nigra/), which are growths, not pigment patches.

Colour gives a clue to depth. Light to dark brown marks sit in the upper skin and respond well. Grey or blue-grey marks sit deeper and fade slowly.

## How to spot it

- The patch is flat: you can see it but not feel it.
- It is darker than your normal skin, with no scale or itch of its own.
- It appeared after a spot, rash, bite, burn, cut, hair removal or a cosmetic procedure, or in sun-exposed skin.
- It darkens further after time in the sun.

A dark spot that is raised, growing, bleeding, itching or changing shape is a different matter and should be examined; see [moles and when to worry](/conditions/moles/).

## Causes and triggers

- **Inflammation:** [acne](/conditions/acne/), eczema, insect bites, infections, ingrown hairs, burns and injuries.
- **Sun and visible light.** Lagos has strong sun all year. Visible light and heat, as well as ultraviolet, darken pigment in dark skin.
- **Friction:** tight clothing, scrubbing with sponges and nets, thighs rubbing, leaning on elbows.
- **Hormones:** pregnancy, contraceptives, thyroid and insulin problems.
- **Products:** fragranced creams, hair dye running onto the skin, harsh soaps, lime and other home remedies.
- **Medicines** that sensitise skin to light or deposit pigment.
- **Procedures:** waxing, threading, peels and laser done too aggressively.

> [!DERM] See a dermatologist if
> - marks have not faded after three months of sun protection
> - new marks keep appearing
> - patches are grey, blue-black or spreading
> - you have used lightening products and your skin is darker or thinner than before
> - a dark spot is raised, changing or bleeding
> - dark, velvety skin has appeared on your neck or in body folds

> [!AVOID] What to avoid
> - Bleaching creams, soaps, pills and injections, and any product without an ingredient list
> - Mixed creams containing steroids
> - Lime, lemon, baking soda, toothpaste and undiluted acids
> - Scrubbing dark areas; friction makes them darker
> - Picking spots and scabs
> - Peels or laser from anyone who has not prepared your skin or done a test patch

## How it is diagnosed at the clinic

The dermatologist examines the skin in daylight and with a Wood's lamp, which helps show how deep the pigment sits, and uses a dermatoscope where needed. You are asked about products, medicines, hormones and sun exposure. Blood tests are arranged if a hormonal or metabolic cause is likely. A small [biopsy](/services/skin-tests-and-diagnostics/skin-biopsy/) is occasionally needed when the diagnosis is unclear.

## Hyperpigmentation treatment: what works

Treatment has four parts, in this order.

**1. Stop the cause.** Control the acne, eczema or ingrown hairs; stop the product or the friction. Without this step everything else is temporary.

**2. Protect from light.** A broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or more every morning, reapplied if you are outdoors. Tinted sunscreens containing iron oxides block visible light and suit dark skin tones. See [Do Black people need sunscreen?](/blog/do-black-people-need-sunscreen/)

**3. Calm the pigment cells.** Prescription creams reduce melanin production. They include hydroquinone used for a set period under supervision, retinoids, and non-hydroquinone options such as azelaic acid. The dermatologist chooses by the type of pigment and your skin's sensitivity, and the clinic's [pharmacy](/services/dermatology-pharmacy/) dispenses them.

**4. Speed up clearance.** A series of [superficial chemical peels](/services/aesthetic-treatments/chemical-peels/) lifts pigment from the upper skin. [Laser](/services/laser-treatments/laser-pigmentation-removal/) is reserved for selected types after a test patch.

Expect gradual change: first the edges soften, then the centre lightens. Photographs taken at the clinic help you see progress that is easy to miss day to day.

## Prevention and daily care

- Sunscreen every morning, and shade or a hat when the sun is high.
- Treat spots and rashes early instead of waiting for marks to form.
- Keep skincare simple: gentle cleanser, moisturiser, sunscreen, one treatment product.
- Introduce new products one at a time and stop anything that stings or burns.
- Wash with your hands or a soft cloth, not a rough sponge.
- After shaving or waxing, moisturise and avoid sun on the area.
