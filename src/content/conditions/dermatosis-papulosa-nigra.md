## What DPN looks like on brown and Black skin

DPN appears as many small, dark brown to black bumps that look as if they have been stuck onto the skin. Early spots are flat or barely raised and can be mistaken for freckles. With time they become dome-shaped, and some develop a tiny stalk.

They are most numerous over the cheekbones and temples and around the eyes, and may spread to the forehead, neck, upper chest and back. The first spots often appear in the teens or twenties and increase through adult life. Many people have a parent with the same spots.

The medical importance of DPN is nil. Its importance to the person is appearance, and occasionally spots catch on a towel, razor or necklace.

## How to spot it

- Many similar small dark bumps, not one or two
- On the upper cheeks, temples and around the eyes
- Slightly raised when you run a finger over them
- No change from week to week; a slow increase over years
- Family members with the same

A flat dark patch is not DPN; see [hyperpigmentation and dark spots](/conditions/hyperpigmentation/).

## Causes and triggers

DPN is an inherited tendency for small overgrowths of the surface layer of skin. It is a variant of seborrhoeic keratosis. It affects a large proportion of adults with dark skin and is more common in women. Sun exposure may contribute. It is not caused by diet, cosmetics or infection, and it is not contagious.

> [!DERM] See a dermatologist if
> - a dark spot is growing fast, bleeding, itching or looks different from the rest
> - many spots appear suddenly over weeks
> - spots are catching on clothing or shaving
> - you would like them removed
> - you have a dark spot on a palm, sole, or under a nail

> [!AVOID] What to avoid
> - Bleaching creams and acids
> - Removal creams, "wart pens" and plasma pens used at home or in salons
> - Thread, blades and burning with hot objects
> - Picking, which leaves dark marks and can cause infection
> - Laser offered without a test spot

## How it is diagnosed at the clinic

DPN is recognised on sight and confirmed with a dermatoscope, which shows the typical pattern of a harmless surface growth. A spot that does not fit the pattern is removed and sent for laboratory examination. See [mole checks and dermoscopy](/services/skin-cancer-care/mole-mapping-and-dermoscopy/).

## DPN removal at the clinic

Treatment is optional. If you choose it, the aim is to remove the spots while disturbing the surrounding skin as little as possible, because injured dark skin pigments.

- **Light electrocautery (electrodesiccation).** A fine tip delivers a very brief, low-energy current that dries the spot, which is then wiped away or left to crust. This is the method used most.
- **Snip or curettage.** Raised or stalked spots are snipped with fine scissors or scraped off.
- **Laser.** An [Erbium YAG](/services/laser-treatments/erbium-yag-laser/) or other laser can vaporise spots precisely in selected cases.
- **Cryotherapy** is generally avoided on dark skin for DPN, because freezing destroys pigment cells and leaves pale marks.

**How a treatment goes.** Numbing cream is applied for 30 to 45 minutes. A small test area is treated first, usually near the hairline, and reviewed after a few weeks to see how your skin heals. If it heals well, the rest are treated over one or more visits. Dozens of spots can be treated in a visit.

**Afterwards.** Each site forms a small dark crust that drops off in five to ten days. Do not pick. Wash gently, apply petroleum jelly, avoid make-up on the area for a few days and use sunscreen daily. Temporary dark or light dots fade over weeks to months.

**Risks.** Temporary or, rarely, lasting dark or pale marks; a tiny scar; infection; incomplete removal. [Keloid scars](/conditions/keloids/) on the face from this procedure are very uncommon.

## Prevention and daily care

- DPN cannot be prevented, but daily sunscreen may slow new spots and protects treated skin.
- Leave the spots alone between treatments.
- Have any spot that changes checked.
