## What contact dermatitis looks like on brown and Black skin

Redness is hard to see on dark skin. Look for a patch that is darker brown, purple or grey than the skin around it, slightly swollen, with tiny bumps or blisters, and intensely itchy. After days of scratching the area becomes dry, thick and lined. When it heals, a dark or pale mark often stays for weeks to months.

The location is the main clue:

- **Hairline, ears, neck and eyelids:** hair dye, relaxer, shampoo, wig or lace-front glue.
- **Eyelids alone:** nail polish, lash glue, eye make-up, or something transferred by the fingers.
- **Earlobes, wrist, waistband, under a belt buckle:** nickel in jewellery and metal fasteners.
- **Hands:** soaps, detergents, bleach, gloves, cement, food handling.
- **Underarms:** deodorant or fragrance.
- **Feet:** rubber and glues in shoes, leather tanning chemicals.
- **Face and neck in a streaky or patchy pattern:** perfumes, plants, essential oils and creams.

## How to spot it

- The rash has a clear border or an odd shape.
- It appeared hours to days after a new product, job or activity.
- It improves on holiday or away from work and returns afterwards.
- It affects the hand you use most, or only one side.

## Causes and triggers

Common irritants are water, soap, washing-up liquid, bleach, solvents and friction. Common allergens are nickel, fragrance, preservatives in creams and wipes, paraphenylenediamine (PPD) in dark hair dye and "black henna", rubber chemicals in gloves and shoes, acrylates in nail products and adhesives, and some medicated creams including antibiotic ointments. Heat and sweat in Lagos help metals and dyes leach onto the skin, and frequent hand washing strips its protective oils.

> [!URGENT] Get urgent medical help if
> - your lips, tongue or throat swell, or you find it hard to breathe
> - your whole face swells and blisters after hair dye

> [!DERM] See a dermatologist if
> - a rash keeps returning and you cannot find the cause
> - your hands are cracked and sore enough to affect work
> - the rash is on your face, eyelids or genitals
> - shop-bought creams have not helped after two weeks

> [!AVOID] What to avoid
> - Re-using the suspected product "to check"
> - Mixed steroid creams and lightening creams on the rash
> - Antiseptic liquids and hot water on cracked hands
> - Dyeing hair again after any reaction, including with "natural" or "ammonia-free" dyes, until you have been tested

## How it is diagnosed at the clinic

The dermatologist asks about your work, hobbies, hair and skin products, and examines where the rash sits. Where an allergy is suspected, [patch testing](/services/skin-tests-and-diagnostics/patch-testing/) is arranged once the rash has settled. A scraping rules out fungal infection, and a [skin biopsy](/services/skin-tests-and-diagnostics/skin-biopsy/) is occasionally needed if the diagnosis is unclear.

## Contact dermatitis treatment at the clinic

- **Avoidance.** You receive the names of what you are allergic to, where they are found and what to use instead.
- **Calming the rash.** A corticosteroid cream of the right strength for the site, used for a set number of days, with a moisturiser many times a day.
- **Hand care.** A soap substitute, cotton-lined gloves for wet work, and moisturiser after every wash.
- **Severe reactions** may need a short course of tablets.
- **Work.** A letter for your employer where adjustments would help. Employers can arrange [corporate skin screening](/services/dermatology-consultations/corporate-skin-screening/) for staff in high-risk jobs.

Care for this condition is part of [clinical dermatology](/services/clinical-dermatology/) at the clinic.

## Prevention and daily care

- Choose fragrance-free products with short ingredient lists.
- Do a small test of new hair dye behind the ear two days before each use, as the pack instructs.
- Cover metal buttons and choose jewellery labelled nickel-free.
- Moisturise your hands after washing and before bed.
