## What melasma looks like on brown and Black skin

Melasma shows as flat patches with irregular but fairly defined edges, darker than the surrounding skin, in a mirror-image pattern on both sides of the face. The colour ranges from tan to deep brown; a grey or bluish tone means pigment lies deeper.

Three patterns are recognised:

- **Centre of the face:** forehead, cheeks, nose, upper lip and chin. The most common.
- **Cheekbones and nose.**
- **Jawline.**

It occasionally affects the forearms and neck. The skin feels normal: no scale, no itch.

On dark skin melasma is often confused with other causes of facial darkening: marks left by acne, darkening from friction or cosmetics, and ochronosis from long-term lightening creams, which looks sooty or speckled. The treatments differ, so the distinction matters; see [hyperpigmentation and dark spots](/conditions/hyperpigmentation/).

## How to spot it

- Patches on both sides of the face in roughly the same places
- Flat and smooth, with no change in skin texture
- Darker after sunny days, cooking over heat or a hot season
- Began or worsened in pregnancy, or on a contraceptive pill, injection or implant
- A mother or sister with the same patches

## Causes and triggers

Melasma arises when pigment cells in certain areas of the face become overactive. The exact reason is not known. Recognised triggers are:

- **Sunlight:** ultraviolet and visible light both drive it. Ordinary sunscreens do not block visible light; tinted ones do.
- **Heat:** hot weather, cooking fires, ovens, steam.
- **Hormones:** pregnancy ("the mask of pregnancy"), hormonal contraception and hormone therapy.
- **Family tendency.**
- **Thyroid disease** in some people.
- **Irritating products,** fragrances and harsh soaps.
- **Some medicines** that sensitise skin to light.

Lagos combines strong year-round sun with heat and humidity, which is why melasma here needs consistent protection, not seasonal protection.

> [!DERM] See a dermatologist if
> - facial patches have lasted more than three months
> - they are spreading or getting darker despite sunscreen
> - you have used lightening creams and the skin is now darker, thinner or red
> - you are unsure whether the patches are melasma
> - you are pregnant or planning pregnancy and want safe options

> [!AVOID] What to avoid
> - Bleaching creams and "toning" products bought without a prescription. Many contain strong steroids, high-dose hydroquinone or mercury.
> - Using a prescribed lightening cream for longer than you were told
> - Scrubs, lime, lemon and home acids
> - Waxing or threading over affected skin
> - Laser or strong peels as a first step
> - Going without sunscreen, even on cloudy days

## How it is diagnosed at the clinic

Melasma is diagnosed by its appearance. A Wood's lamp shows whether the pigment is mainly in the upper or deeper skin, which predicts how well it will respond. The dermatologist asks about pregnancy, contraception, medicines and the creams you have used, and may check thyroid function. A [biopsy](/services/skin-tests-and-diagnostics/skin-biopsy/) is needed only when ochronosis or another condition is suspected.

## Melasma treatment: a long-term plan

**Light protection, every day.** A tinted, broad-spectrum sunscreen containing iron oxides, applied generously each morning and again at midday, with a hat and shade outdoors. Without this, no other treatment holds.

**Prescription creams.** The dermatologist chooses from pigment-suppressing creams, used alone or as a triple combination for a limited number of weeks. Treatment then steps down to a maintenance cream. Stopping suddenly invites rebound, so the plan includes how to reduce.

**Tablets.** Tranexamic acid by mouth helps some people with stubborn melasma. It is not suitable for anyone with a history of blood clots or certain other conditions, and it is prescribed only after assessment.

**Peels.** A series of [superficial chemical peels](/services/aesthetic-treatments/chemical-peels/) can speed lightening once the skin has been prepared.

**Laser.** Considered last; see [laser pigmentation removal](/services/laser-treatments/laser-pigmentation-removal/).

**In pregnancy and breastfeeding,** treatment is limited to sun protection and options the doctor confirms are safe.

Improvement is usually visible in two to three months. Expect control, with good and bad spells, not a cure.

## Prevention and daily care

- Tinted sunscreen every morning, indoors and out.
- A wide-brimmed hat or umbrella between 10 am and 4 pm.
- Stand back from cooking heat; ventilate the kitchen.
- Use a gentle cleanser and fragrance-free moisturiser.
- Discuss contraception options with your doctor if melasma began on hormones.
- Keep follow-up appointments, so that treatment can be stepped down safely.
