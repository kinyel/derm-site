## What phototherapy is

Phototherapy treats skin disease with ultraviolet (UV) light from special lamps. The light calms overactive immune cells in the skin and, in vitiligo, stimulates pigment cells in the hair follicles and at the edges of patches to multiply and move back into white skin.

The clinic's equipment includes a **full-body phototherapy booth** and a **digital handheld phototherapy unit**.

## The forms of phototherapy

**Narrowband UVB.** The standard form today. The lamps emit a narrow band of light around 311 nanometres. No tablets or creams are needed with it, and it is used in children and in pregnancy when a dermatologist advises.

**Full-body booth.** You stand inside a cabinet lined with lamps for a set number of seconds or minutes. It treats the whole skin surface at once and is used for widespread [vitiligo](/conditions/vitiligo/), [psoriasis](/conditions/psoriasis/), [eczema](/conditions/eczema/) and generalised itching.

**Handheld unit.** A small lamp treats limited areas, such as a few patches of vitiligo, the scalp, hands or feet, without exposing the rest of the skin.

**Excimer light.** A high-intensity, targeted beam of UVB at 308 nanometres for small, stubborn patches. Because it treats only the affected skin, higher doses can be used and fewer treatments may be needed.

**PUVA.** Psoralen, a medicine that makes skin sensitive to light, is taken as a tablet or applied to the skin, followed by UVA. It penetrates deeper and is kept for thick plaques on palms and soles and for conditions that have not responded to UVB.

## Phototherapy for vitiligo

Vitiligo matters differently on dark skin. The contrast between white patches and brown skin is stark, and in Nigeria the condition still carries stigma and is sometimes confused with leprosy. That makes effective treatment more than cosmetic.

What to expect:

- **Colour returns as freckles** around hair follicles, which then spread and join.
- **The face and neck respond best,** then the trunk and limbs. Fingertips, lips, feet and patches with white hair respond least.
- **Treatment is long.** Six to twelve months is common, sometimes longer.
- **Creams are used alongside** to improve the result.
- **Some colour can be lost again** after stopping, so follow-up continues.

## Before, during and after

**Before.** The dermatologist confirms the diagnosis, checks your medicines for any that increase light sensitivity, and examines your skin. Your starting dose is set from your skin type or from a small test dose. You are asked not to apply perfumed products or certain creams before treatment.

**During.** You undress to the agreed extent, put on protective goggles and, for men, genital protection. The face is shielded if it does not need treatment. The lamps come on for the calculated time. The skin feels warm. A visit takes about 10 to 15 minutes in total.

**After.** Mild pinkness or warmth a few hours later is expected and guides the next dose. Moisturise daily. Protect treated skin from extra sun on treatment days. Tell the staff at each visit about any redness, soreness or new medicines.

## Risks and how they are managed

| Possible effect | What is done |
|---|---|
| Redness or burning like sunburn | Dose reduced; cool compresses, moisturiser |
| Dry, itchy skin | Regular moisturiser |
| Tanning of surrounding skin | Expected; fades after the course |
| Cold sore flare | Lips protected with sunscreen; antiviral treatment |
| Eye damage | Prevented by goggles |
| Skin ageing and cancer with very high lifetime doses | Cumulative dose recorded; skin checked regularly |

Phototherapy is not suitable for people with lupus, some light-sensitive conditions, a history of melanoma or certain inherited disorders. PUVA is avoided in pregnancy.

> [!DERM] Tell the phototherapy team if
> - your skin stayed red or sore for more than a day after a treatment
> - you have started any new tablet, cream or herbal product
> - you will miss treatments, so that the dose can be adjusted when you return
