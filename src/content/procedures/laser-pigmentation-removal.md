## Diagnosis comes before laser

"Dark spots" is a description, not a diagnosis. The causes behave differently under a laser:

| Type of pigment | Typical cause | Response to laser |
|---|---|---|
| Marks after acne, bites or rashes (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation) | Inflammation | Variable; creams and peels first |
| [Melasma](/conditions/melasma/) | Hormones, sun, heat, visible light | Often relapses or worsens |
| Sun spots (solar lentigines) | Years of sun | Usually good |
| Small raised dark spots ([dermatosis papulosa nigra](/conditions/dermatosis-papulosa-nigra/)) | Inherited | Removed by fine cautery or laser |
| Some birthmarks (for example naevus of Ota) | Present from birth or teens | Good, over many treatments |
| Darkening from bleaching creams (ochronosis) | Long-term hydroquinone misuse | Difficult; specialist plan |
| Dark, velvety skin folds | Insulin resistance, friction | Not a laser condition |

The dermatologist examines the pigment, often with a dermatoscope and a Wood's lamp, to judge its type and depth. See [hyperpigmentation and dark spots](/conditions/hyperpigmentation/) for the full picture.

## How pigment lasers work

Pigment lasers deliver energy in bursts lasting billionths or trillionths of a second. The burst is so brief that pigment particles shatter before heat spreads to the surrounding skin. The fragments are then cleared by the body's own cells over the following weeks.

The clinic's [Q-switched laser](/services/laser-treatments/q-switch-laser/) and [pico laser](/services/laser-treatments/pico-laser/) both work this way. Our article [Pico laser or Q-switch laser for dark spots?](/blog/pico-laser-or-q-switch-laser-for-dark-spots/) explains the difference.

## Laser dark spot removal on brown and Black skin

The pigment being targeted and the pigment that gives skin its colour are the same substance. On dark skin the aim is to treat the excess without provoking the normal pigment cells. That means low energy, a longer wavelength, wider spacing between treatments and patience. A plan that promises fast clearance on dark skin should be treated with suspicion.

Preparation matters as much as the laser: four weeks of sunscreen, often with a prescription cream that settles pigment production, before and after treatment.

## Before, during and after

**Before.** A consultation, photographs with your consent, and a test patch reviewed after four weeks. Avoid sun, waxing and irritating products on the area. Tell the doctor about cold sores and any bleaching products.

**During.** Eye shields are worn. The laser makes a snapping sound and feels like hot pinpricks. Treated spots may turn briefly white or grey. A small area takes a few minutes.

**After.** Spots darken and may form a thin crust that falls off in five to ten days. Do not pick. Keep the skin moisturised, avoid sun and heat, and use sunscreen. Pigment creams are restarted when the doctor advises.

## Risks and how they are managed

- **Temporary darkening (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation):** the most common problem on dark skin; treated with sunscreen and pigment creams.
- **Pale spots or patches:** can follow repeated or high-energy treatment and may be slow to recover or permanent. Conservative settings and limits on the number of treatments reduce this.
- **Blisters, crusting, scarring:** uncommon with correct settings.
- **Incomplete clearance or return of pigment.**

> [!DERM] Tell the doctor before laser if
> - your dark patches appeared in pregnancy or on the pill (possible melasma)
> - you have used lightening creams, especially for months or years
> - a spot has changed in size, shape or colour, or bleeds
> - you have had a bad reaction to laser or a peel before
