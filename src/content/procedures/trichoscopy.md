## What the dermatologist looks for

- **Hair shaft thickness.** A mix of thick and thin hairs means follicles are shrinking, the mark of pattern hair loss.
- **Follicle openings.** Their absence in a smooth patch means the follicle has been destroyed by scarring.
- **Broken and tapered hairs.** "Exclamation mark" hairs point to [alopecia areata](/conditions/alopecia-areata/); short broken hairs at the edges suggest [traction](/conditions/traction-alopecia/).
- **Scale, redness and pustules** around follicles, which signal inflammation or infection.
- **Hair shaft defects,** such as knots and fractures, behind [hair breakage](/conditions/hair-breakage/).

## Other hair tests

- **Hair pull test:** a small bundle of hairs is gently pulled to see how many come away; more than a few suggests active shedding.
- **Hair shaft microscopy:** plucked or broken hairs are examined under a microscope for structural faults.
- **Hair density assessment:** hairs are counted in a marked area to measure thinning and track treatment.
- **Blood tests:** iron, thyroid and, where indicated, hormones.

## On African-textured hair

The normal scalp of people with dark skin looks different under the dermatoscope: a honeycomb pigment pattern and pinpoint white dots are normal findings, not signs of disease. Tightly curled hair breaks more easily and grows from curved follicles. Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia, a scarring hair loss that starts at the crown, mainly affects women of African descent and is easy to miss early on without trichoscopy. Knowing these patterns is what makes the test reliable.
