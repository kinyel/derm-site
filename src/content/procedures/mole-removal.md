## Why moles are removed

- **For diagnosis.** A mole that looks unusual is removed so that a pathologist can examine it.
- **For comfort.** A raised mole that catches on a bra strap, collar, comb or razor.
- **By choice,** because of how it looks.

Before anything is removed, the dermatologist examines the mole with a dermatoscope and decides whether it is an ordinary mole, another harmless growth, or something that must be tested. Many "moles" people ask about are other things: [skin tags](/conditions/skin-tags/), seborrhoeic keratoses or the small dark spots of [dermatosis papulosa nigra](/conditions/dermatosis-papulosa-nigra/), each with its own method of removal. See [moles and when to worry](/conditions/moles/).

## How moles are removed

**Shave removal.** After numbing, a blade removes the raised part of the mole level with the surrounding skin. The base is sealed with light cautery. No stitches are needed. It suits raised, clearly harmless moles and leaves a flat mark.

**Surgical excision.** The mole is cut out in full with a small margin of normal skin, and the wound is closed with stitches. This is the method for any mole that might be abnormal, for flat moles, and when the whole mole must be examined. It leaves a line scar a little longer than the mole was wide.

**Punch excision.** A circular blade removes a small mole in one piece; one or two stitches close the wound.

Laser and freezing are not used for moles that have not been tested, because they destroy the tissue that the laboratory needs.

## Laboratory examination

Any mole with a feature of concern, and any mole removed by excision, is sent for histology. The report usually takes one to two weeks. It confirms what the mole was and whether it has been completely removed. If the result is abnormal, the dermatologist explains what it means and whether a wider excision is needed; see [skin cancer care](/services/skin-cancer-care/).

## Mole removal on brown and Black skin

Two things differ. First, pigment: the scar often heals darker than the surrounding skin and takes six to twelve months to blend in; sun protection helps. Second, raised scars: [keloid scars](/conditions/keloids/) are more common in people of African descent. If you or close relatives form keloids, tell the dermatologist. The risk depends on the body site: it is highest on the chest, shoulders, upper back, jawline and earlobes, and low on the face, palms and soles. The plan may include a different technique, pressure, silicone or early steroid injection, or a decision to leave a harmless mole alone.

Moles on the palms, soles and nails of people with dark skin deserve particular attention and are always assessed carefully before removal.

## Before, during and after

**Before.** Tell the doctor about blood thinners, allergies, a pacemaker and keloids. Eat normally. Photographs may be taken for your record with your consent.

**During.** The skin is cleaned, marked and numbed. Removal takes a few minutes. The wound is dressed and you are shown how to look after it.

**After.**
- Keep the dressing dry for 24 hours.
- Then wash gently each day, apply petroleum jelly and a clean dressing.
- Stitches are removed after 5 to 7 days on the face and 10 to 14 days on the body.
- Avoid stretching the area, swimming and heavy exercise until healed.
- Protect the scar from the sun for several months.

## Risks and how they are managed

- **Bleeding or bruising:** firm pressure for 15 minutes; contact the clinic if it continues.
- **Infection:** uncommon; treated with antibiotics.
- **Scar, dark or light mark:** expected to a degree; fades over months.
- **Keloid or raised scar:** risk assessed beforehand; treated early if it starts.
- **Regrowth** after shave removal.
- **Need for further surgery** if the laboratory result requires it.

> [!DERM] Contact the clinic after removal if
> - the wound bleeds through the dressing
> - pain, redness, swelling or pus develop
> - the scar begins to thicken, itch or rise
